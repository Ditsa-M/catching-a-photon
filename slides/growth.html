We have the checklist, and we have a gap of one to two orders of magnitude. How has the field tried to close it? The timeline on the right tells the story in two colours: amber for photochemistry, teal for AI. It starts in 1972, when Fujishima and Honda split water on TiO2 with light. In 2001 the Photosystem I structure gave us nature’s blueprint. In 2007 Behler and Parrinello showed that neural networks can learn quantum-mechanical energies. By 2020 the two threads meet: a mobile robot ran its own photocatalyst search, and SrTiO3:Al reached near-unity quantum efficiency, but only in the UV. 2021 brought 100 m² of panels making hydrogen, at only 0.76 percent. 2023 brought a 9.2 percent lab record and the big AI materials projects. The chart on the left is real data from OpenAlex: papers combining machine learning with photocatalysis went from 14 in 2015 to 361 in 2025, about 26-fold, doubling every 1.8 years. Why did chemists turn to AI so fast? Because of the size of the search problem.
