At 77 K, red emission reports where the energy is stuck

Why freeze

At 77 K uphill transfer stops (previous slide), so red pigments keep their energy long enough to fluoresce. The F760 band becomes a direct readout.

Φ_F760 = k_F / (k_F + k_nr + k_Q)

k_Q: quenching by the reaction centre

A redox reporter

In Spirulina trimers, P700⁺ quenches F760. So the red band’s size depends on the reaction centre’s redox state.

[12:04–13:34] That makes the red chlorophylls a built-in probe. At room temperature emission is weak and broad. Freeze to 77 K and a strong band appears at 760 nm in the Spirulina trimer, F760, from energy stuck on the red pigments. Its yield is k_F over the sum of k_F, k_nr and k_Q, where k_Q is quenching by the reaction centre. The key published observation is that P700+, the oxidised special pair, quenches F760. So the size of the red band depends on the redox state of the reaction centre. The spectra here are a schematic model, but the logic is the one used in the literature: low-temperature emission reads out what the trap is doing. Two practical points about this measurement. First, at 77 K the sample is a glass, so the redox state you prepared at room temperature is locked in when it freezes. Second, the yield equation tells you what to compare: if k_F and k_nr stay the same, any change in F760 means a change in k_Q, which means a change at the reaction centre. Normalising to a band that does not respond, such as the bulk emission, removes differences in sample concentration.
