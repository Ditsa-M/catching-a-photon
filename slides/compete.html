One state, many exits

An excitation on a red pigment can fluoresce, decay as heat, or be removed by the reaction centre. Change one exit and the others change too.

What the model predicts

Making the trap less effective should brighten F760 and lengthen its lifetime. Lifetime measurements can test this separately from yield.

[22:13–23:36] What should we expect? Put the red chlorophyll into the rate-competition picture. Its fluorescence yield is k_F over k_F plus k_nr plus k_Q. With illustrative numbers, a radiative lifetime of 15 ns and a non-radiative one of 5 ns, the curve shows the yield against quenching by the reaction centre. When the trap removes energy quickly, the red emission is dim and short-lived. When the trap is closed, k_Q falls, and the yield and lifetime both rise. This is the Stern–Volmer form: Φ0 over Φ equals 1 plus k_Q τ0. The prediction is testable two ways: brightness and fluorescence lifetime, which don't depend on sample concentration. Put numbers in. With no quenching, Φ is 0.067 over 0.267, about 0.25, and the lifetime is about 3.8 ns. With k_Q at one per nanosecond, Φ drops to about 0.05 and the lifetime to under a nanosecond. That's a fivefold change in brightness from a change in one rate. In real PSI the trap rate is much faster than this, so even small changes in how well the trap works should show up clearly.
