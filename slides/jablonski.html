The excited state lives for nanoseconds

Kasha’s rule

Upper states relax in femtoseconds, so emission, transfer and chemistry start from the lowest S₁: for Chl, the Qy state.

Gouterman four‑orbital model

Two HOMOs and two LUMOs mix into a strong Soret band (≈ 430 nm) and weaker Q bands (Qy ≈ 670 nm). The protein tunes Qy.

Φ_F = kᵣ / (kᵣ + k_IC + k_ISC + k_EET)

Chl a: τ ≈ 6 ns, Φ_F ≈ 0.32

→ kᵣ = Φ_F/τ ≈ 5 × 10⁷ s⁻¹

[4:20–5:30] Once excited, what can the molecule do? Walk the Jablonski diagram. One: absorption into S2, the Soret band near 430 nm, in femtoseconds. Two: internal conversion to S1 within about 200 fs; that is Kasha's rule, so whatever you excite, the chemistry starts from the lowest singlet, for chlorophyll the Qy state. Three: fluorescence. In solution chlorophyll a lives about 6 ns with a quantum yield of 0.32, so the radiative rate is about 5 × 10^7 per second. Four: intersystem crossing to the triplet, which can sensitise singlet oxygen and cause damage; hold that thought for the Dexter slide. Five: in the protein, transfer to a neighbour in under a picosecond beats every loss channel. Gouterman's four-orbital model explains the strong Soret and weaker Q bands, and why the protein can tune Qy. So the clock is about 6 ns, and only transfer saves the energy.
