Some chlorophylls sit below the trap

Low-energy “red” pigments

Strongly coupled chlorophyll clusters absorb at 708–740 nm, below P700 at 700 nm. Cyanobacterial PSI trimers have several, and the Spirulina trimer has one at ~740 nm.

k_up/k_down = (g_up/g_down) e^(−ΔE/kT)

Detailed balance

At room temperature heat lifts energy back to P700. Freeze to 77 K and it stays trapped on the red pigments.

[10:25–12:04] Cyanobacterial PSI contains red chlorophylls: strongly coupled clusters absorbing at 708 to 740 nm, below the trap at 700 nm. The Spirulina trimer has one near 740 nm. On the diagram the red level sits about 96 meV below P700. Going downhill to it is fast. Getting back up needs heat, and detailed balance from lecture 13 sets the ratio: k_up over k_down equals e to the minus ΔE over kT. At room temperature kT is 25 meV, so the ratio is about 0.02, slower than downhill but fast enough, and the energy still reaches P700. Cool to 77 K and kT drops to 6.6 meV: the ratio falls to about 5 × 10^-7. Uphill transfer stops. Energy that lands on a red chlorophyll at 77 K stays there. Why do red chlorophylls exist at all if they slow trapping? One proposal is that they extend absorption into the far-red, where shaded cells still get light, and they may help protect the reaction centre by holding energy when it is closed. Either way, the ratio of 0.02 at room temperature tells us why the cost is small: red sites slow trapping somewhat but barely lower its yield. At 77 K the balance is completely different, and that's what makes them useful as a probe.
