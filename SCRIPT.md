# Catching a photon: speaker script (≈30 min slot)

Story: open with a zoom from a plant down to one π electron (slide 2), then climb back out: excited molecule → pigment pairs → antenna and reaction centre → leaf and artificial leaf. Times assume ~130 words/min; the talk ends near **26:50**, leaving about 3 minutes for questions. Each block starts with [start–end] times.

Total: 3515 words.

## 1. Catching a photon before it’s lost

[0:00–0:38] An excited molecule has about six nanoseconds before its energy is lost as heat or light. Today I follow one photon through that window. We start where photosynthesis starts, with a plant, and zoom in until we reach a single electron. Then we climb back out one level at a time: the excited molecule, the antenna proteins that move its energy, the reaction centre that turns it into charge, and finally the artificial leaf, and how machine learning is helping to design one.

## 2. From a leaf to one electron: nine levels of zoom

[0:38–2:07] Here is the zoom. A plant stores about one percent of the sunlight that reaches it. Zoom into a leaf: a thin, wet solar panel. Inside, palisade cells are packed with chloroplasts lining the walls. Each chloroplast holds stacks of thylakoid membranes, the grana, sitting in the stroma. Zoom into one membrane and you reach the machinery: Photosystem II, cytochrome b6f, Photosystem I and ATP synthase, with plastoquinone and plastocyanin shuttling between them. Zoom into Photosystem I: about 96 chlorophylls per monomer form an antenna. Watch one excitation hop to the special pair, P700, and an electron leave through the iron–sulfur clusters. Zoom into one chlorophyll: a magnesium ion in a conjugated macrocycle with a phytol tail. And at the bottom, the electrons. One red photon, about 1.8 eV, moves one π electron from the HOMO to the LUMO in femtoseconds. That is where the story starts. The ladder on the right is the map for the talk: slides 3 to 6 stay at the electron, 7 to 17 work at the level of pigment pairs and proteins, and from slide 18 we zoom back out to the leaf, and to artificial ones.

## 3. Sunlight is abundant, but each photon is weak

[2:07–3:18] First the budget for that photon, at the scale of the planet. Start with the prize. The Sun delivers about 1.2 × 10^5 terawatts to Earth; humanity used about 13.5 in 2001. Supply is not the problem; conversion is. E = hc/λ: 1239.8 divided by the wavelength in nanometres gives electronvolts, so a 680 nm photon carries 1.82 eV, 176 kJ per mole. The dashed line is 1.23 eV, the thermodynamic minimum per electron for splitting water, and you need four electrons per O2, so the readout never drops below four photons. C–H and O–H bonds are 4.3 to 4.8 eV, bigger than any visible photon. So photosynthesis works one electron at a time: eight photons move four electrons from water at +0.82 V to NADP+ at −0.32 V, storing about 4.6 of the 14.4 eV absorbed, roughly 32 percent. The rest buys speed and irreversibility, an idea that comes back at every step.

## 4. Light opens reaction paths that heat cannot

[3:18–4:43] Now the bottom of the zoom: the electron. Why is photochemistry different from thermal chemistry? Three points from lecture 14. One: activation by light instead of heat changes selectivity. Two: the excited state sits 2 to 3 eV higher, so many more product states are thermodynamically reachable. Three: excited states are electronic isomers of the ground state, with a new electron distribution and often a new geometry. The animation is the textbook case, butadiene closing to cyclobutene. Thermally the HOMO is ψ2, whose terminal lobes have opposite phase, so both ends must rotate the same way, conrotatory, to overlap in phase. After absorbing light the HOMO is ψ3, with matching terminal phases, so the ring closes disrotatory. Same molecule, opposite stereochemistry: Woodward–Hoffmann. Biology shows the downside: UV-B makes thymine [2+2] dimers in DNA, photochemically allowed and thermally forbidden. And the redox point matters for the rest of this talk: an excited molecule is both a better oxidant and a better reductant. For P700, +0.45 V minus 1.77 eV gives about −1.3 V. Light turns a mild donor into one of the strongest reductants in biology.

## 5. Electrons jump, nuclei wait: Franck–Condon

[4:43–5:51] How does absorption actually happen? Electrons move far faster than nuclei, so the transition is vertical: the molecule lands on the excited surface at the ground-state geometry. That is the Franck–Condon principle from lectures 15 and 16. In the animation the excited potential is displaced by Δ. The bars are the vibronic progression; each intensity is the squared overlap of vibrational wavefunctions, which for displaced harmonic oscillators is a Poisson distribution with Huang–Rhys factor S, about Δ squared over 2. Small displacement: nearly everything in the 0–0 line. Large displacement: the band broadens toward higher vibrational levels. The energy the nuclei dump as they relax, S times ħω, is a reorganisation energy, λ. Remember that symbol. In lecture 16 the same Franck–Condon factor appears in electron transfer, where at high temperature it becomes the Marcus exponential. One overlap idea controls absorption, emission, energy transfer and electron transfer.

## 6. The excited state lives for nanoseconds

[5:51–7:04] Once excited, what can the molecule do? Walk the Jablonski diagram. One: absorption into S2, the Soret band near 430 nm, in femtoseconds. Two: internal conversion to S1 within about 200 fs; that is Kasha's rule, so whatever you excite, the chemistry starts from the lowest singlet, for chlorophyll the Qy state. Three: fluorescence. In solution chlorophyll a lives about 6 ns with a quantum yield of 0.32, so the radiative rate is about 5 × 10^7 per second. Four: intersystem crossing to the triplet, which can sensitise singlet oxygen and cause damage; hold that thought for the Dexter slide. Five: in the protein, transfer to a neighbour in under a picosecond beats every loss channel. Gouterman's four-orbital model explains the strong Soret and weaker Q bands, and why the protein can tune Qy. So the clock is about 6 ns, and only transfer saves the energy. But how often does a chlorophyll even get a photon?

## 7. A lone chlorophyll absorbs ~10 photons per second

[7:04–8:01] Zoom out one level, from one chlorophyll to the protein around it. Photons are rare at the molecular scale. A rate is cross-section times intensity: about 10^−16 square centimetres times 10^17 photons per square centimetre per second in full sun gives roughly ten photons per second per chlorophyll, the textbook figure. On the left, one chlorophyll, slowed twenty times, mostly waiting. A reaction centre is a large, expensive protein; one per pigment would sit idle. Nature's fix is the antenna: about a hundred pigments share one reaction centre, multiplying its photon supply about a hundredfold. The catch is that the energy now lands somewhere in the antenna and has to reach the centre well inside 6 ns. So what do real antenna proteins look like?

## 8. Antenna proteins build an energy funnel

[8:01–9:24] The clearest example is the purple bacterial membrane from lecture 15. LH2 rings, solved for Rhodopseudomonas acidophila, have nine αβ pairs holding 18 B850 and 9 B800 bacteriochlorophylls plus carotenoids. They surround LH1, a larger ring absorbing at 875 nm, which encircles the reaction centre, P870. Watch an excitation: B800 to B850 in under a picosecond, LH2 to LH1 in a few picoseconds, LH1 to the reaction centre in about 35. Each step goes downhill, from 1.55 toward 1.42 eV. That is lecture 15's equation: D* plus A gives D plus A* plus heat, and the heat is the price of irreversibility. Lecture 13 gives the math: kinetics must collapse to thermodynamics, so k forward over k back equals the equilibrium constant, e to the ΔE over kT. B850 to B875 is about 42 meV, 1.6 kT, so the back rate is about a fifth of the forward rate before degeneracy. And P870 sits slightly above B875: the final trapping step is slightly uphill, paid for by thermal energy. A funnel, not a cliff. How does each hop work?

## 9. Excitonic coupling between neighbouring pigments

[9:24–10:26] Zoom in slightly, to two chlorophylls about a nanometre apart. Hops are driven by coupling between transition dipoles. Two pigments interact with energy V, set by μ squared, the distance cubed and an orientation factor κ. The coupled states are symmetric and antisymmetric combinations split by 2V, and only the in-phase one is bright. Head-to-tail, κ is negative, the bright state drops and the band red-shifts: a J-aggregate. Side-by-side it blue-shifts: an H-aggregate. At the magic angle, 54.7 degrees, the coupling vanishes. Worked example: chlorophyll Qy is about 4 debye; 0.9 nm apart gives V around −110 wavenumbers, enough for sub-picosecond exchange. B850 in LH2 is exactly this, a tightly coupled ring whose band is red-shifted by coupling. The same physics colours dye aggregates and stacked COF layers, which return in the machine-learning part.

## 10. Transfer outcompetes excited‑state decay

[10:26–11:26] When coupling is weak compared with disorder and reorganisation, energy hops incoherently: Förster theory. The rate goes as κ squared, the donor's radiative rate and the spectral overlap J, over R to the sixth. The efficiency, k_ET over the sum of all rates, becomes 1 over 1 plus (R over R0) to the sixth. Neighbouring pigments sit about 1 nm apart, far inside R0, so every hop is essentially lossless. When coupling dominates you get delocalised excitons that oscillate, the teal curve; real antennas sit in between, with coupled clusters hopping to each other. The bottom line is rate competition: trapping in about 25 ps against loss in 6 ns gives a yield of 99.6 percent, the number an artificial system has to match. But Förster is not the only mechanism.

## 11. Förster vs Dexter: dipoles or electron exchange

[11:26–12:40] Lecture 16 contrasts two mechanisms. Förster is Coulombic: the donor relaxes and the acceptor is excited at the same time through dipole coupling, with no electrons exchanged, so it works over several nanometres. Dexter is a double electron exchange: the excited electron hops from the donor LUMO to the acceptor LUMO while an electron from the acceptor HOMO moves back to the donor. It needs wavefunction overlap, so the rate decays as e to the minus 2R over L and dies beyond about a nanometre; the plot shows the crossover. Why does it matter in biology? Spin. Triplet energy transfer, T1 plus S0 going to S0 plus T1, is forbidden by the dipole mechanism but allowed by exchange. That is why carotenoids sit in van der Waals contact with chlorophylls: they take the triplet energy by Dexter transfer before it can make singlet oxygen, then release it as heat. Photoprotection is a distance-engineering problem. Back to the full antenna.

## 12. 96 chlorophylls, one reaction centre

[12:40–13:32] Zoom back out to the whole protein, the PSI from the opening animation. Photosystem I puts this together. Each monomer has 12 proteins, 96 chlorophylls and 22 carotenoids, all feeding one trap, P700. The animation is a toy random walk that alternates between a 24-site and a 96-site antenna at the same density. Four times the antenna gives four times the photons, but the walk to the trap gets longer by the factor shown: a real trade-off between collecting light and delivering it. Because each hop is sub-picosecond, even the full antenna finishes in tens of picoseconds, far inside 6 ns. Measured PSI trapping times are 20 to 50 ps. Can we watch it?

## 13. Trapping resolved by transient absorption

[13:32–14:22] Pump–probe spectroscopy: a femtosecond pump starts the clock and a delayed probe measures the change in absorption. Scan the delay and you get ΔA versus wavelength and time. The animation is a schematic model with PSI's shape. Near 680 nm the antenna bleach recovers as excitations leave. Near 820 nm a positive band grows in: the oxidised special pair, P700+, the first sign that energy has become charge. Both share one lifetime of a few tens of picoseconds. Global analysis fits the whole map as decay-associated spectra times exponentials and separates transfer from trapping. So energy is trapped in about 30 ps. What happens inside the reaction centre?

## 14. Reaction centres: a two‑branch cofactor wire

[14:22–15:46] Now zoom into the centre of that protein. Reaction centres are cofactor wires built in two branches with pseudo-two-fold symmetry. The purple bacterial reaction centre was the first membrane protein structure, by Deisenhofer, Michel and Huber in 1985, Nobel Prize 1988. At the top is the special pair, an excitonically coupled dimer: coupling lowers its excited-state energy so it acts as a trap, lowers its oxidation potential, and spreads the hole over two chlorophylls. In this type II centre the electron uses only one branch: special pair to accessory bacteriochlorophyll to pheophytin in about 3 ps, to quinone QA in about 200 ps, then to QB in about 100 microseconds. The animation then switches to type I, Photosystem I: the acceptors end in iron–sulfur clusters and both branches are active, with A1 to FX taking about 20 ns on one side and 200 ns on the other. Type II centres use quinones, as in purple bacteria and PSII; type I use Fe–S clusters, as in PSI and green sulfur bacteria. So why do electrons move so fast and so selectively through protein?

## 15. Tunneling through protein: Dutton’s ruler

[15:46–16:54] They tunnel. Lecture 16's Fermi golden rule gives k = 2π over ħ times the electronic coupling squared times a Franck–Condon factor. The coupling decays exponentially with distance, with β about 1.4 per ångström in protein. Dutton turned that into a ruler: log10 k equals 13 minus 0.6 times (R minus 3.6), minus 3.1 times (ΔG° + λ) squared over λ, with R the edge-to-edge distance in ångströms and energies in eV. The 0.6 is just β over ln 10, so every 1.7 ångströms costs a factor of ten. On the plot: at 5 Å picoseconds, at 14 Å microseconds, at 25 Å about a second. Natural redox chains keep cofactors within about 14 Å, Page and Dutton's rule. Gray and Winkler, attaching ruthenium complexes to proteins, showed that the fold in between tunes the decay. The second term in the ruler is the energetics, which is Marcus.

## 16. Charge separation and Marcus theory

[16:54–18:05] This is transition-state theory again, from lecture 13. Eyring writes k = (k_BT/h) e to the −ΔG‡/RT. Marcus supplies ΔG‡ for electron transfer: (ΔG° + λ) squared over 4λ, where λ is the reorganisation energy, the energy to distort reactants and solvent into the product geometry without moving the electron. The animation drives ΔG° more negative. The crossing point slides down to the reactant minimum, the activationless case where −ΔG° equals λ and the rate is maximal, then climbs back up: the inverted region, where more driving force makes the reaction slower. That answers lecture 16's question: the rate does not keep rising with driving force. Nature's design rule: forward steps near the top of the parabola, wasteful recombination with a huge driving force pushed into the inverted region. Reiter's MD/QM/MM simulations of all 96 PSI chlorophylls show how expensive it is even to compute the energies this needs. Let's put numbers on PSI.

## 17. Photosystem I’s electron staircase

[18:05–19:18] This is the PSI energy–kinetic diagram from lecture 16, with approximate potentials from Brettel's review. The photon lifts P700 from about +0.45 V to the excited state near −1.3 V. Then the electron walks down a staircase: to A0, a chlorophyll, in a few picoseconds; to A1, phylloquinone, in about 30; to the iron–sulfur cluster FX in 20 to 200 ns; to FA and FB in under a microsecond; and to ferredoxin in microseconds. Why not one big jump? Dutton's ruler: one long tunnel would be hopelessly slow. Short hops under 14 Å, each with driving force near λ, are fast. A0 is the redox mediator between P700* and A1 that makes this possible, and each step outruns recombination by orders of magnitude. The cost: 1.77 eV absorbed, about 0.87 eV stored between P700+ and ferredoxin. Nature spends about half the energy to buy a quantum yield near one. Can we build something that does the same?

## 18. Design principles and the efficiency gap

[19:18–20:25] Now zoom all the way back out, to the leaf and to the devices that try to copy it. Everything so far reduces to four steps, and an artificial photocatalyst needs the same four. Absorb: nature shares one reaction centre among a hundred pigments; a photocatalyst needs a band gap above 1.23 eV plus overpotentials, realistically 1.8 to 2.4 eV. Transfer: nature uses sub-picosecond hops; a material needs carriers to reach the surface before they recombine. Separate: nature uses a Marcus-tuned staircase; we use heterojunctions, donor–acceptor frameworks and cocatalysts. Catalyse: biology has ferredoxin, FNR and PSII's manganese cluster; we need cocatalysts that survive photocorrosion. The bars are the reality check: crops about 1 percent, the best scaled panels 0.76 percent solar to hydrogen, a 9.2 percent lab record, and the single-junction limit near 33. Different metrics, same message: one to two orders of magnitude short.

## 19. Growth of machine learning in photocatalysis

[20:25–21:04] How is the field trying to close that gap? The timeline runs from Fujishima and Honda's TiO2 in 1972, through the PSI structure in 2001 and neural-network potentials in 2007, to a robot running its own photocatalyst search in 2020 and 100 square metres of panels in 2021. The chart is real OpenAlex data: papers combining machine learning and photocatalysis went from 14 in 2015 to 361 in 2025, about 26-fold, doubling every 1.8 years. Why the rush? The size of the search problem.

## 20. The search space outgrows quantum chemistry

[21:04–22:00] Remember the cost of simulating one protein; now multiply by every candidate material. The funnel is the workflow most of these papers share: tens of thousands of hypothetical structures, a fast ML prescreen, hybrid DFT on survivors, and a handful made in the lab. Anselmi and colleagues screened more than 20,000 MOFs for CO2 photoreduction: about a month by hybrid DFT, about 4.5 hours with a graph transformer, roughly 160 times faster, and accounting for the model's own error raised recovery of true DFT hits from 20 to 70 percent. The caution: scale is not discovery. GNoME predicted 381,000 stable crystals and A-Lab reported 41 of 58 targets, but a re-analysis disputed many phase assignments. Validation is the theme of the next slides.

## 21. Descriptors mirroring the photosynthetic chain

[22:00–22:53] A fast filter is only as good as what it is asked to look for. Hu and colleagues, Nature Catalysis 2025, built descriptors for the three coupled steps of a molecular CO2-reduction system: photosensitisation, electron transfer and catalysis. That is the same chain as the reaction centre; the dashed lines map each biological step onto the artificial one. They screened 3,444 photosensitiser–catalyst systems and about 180,000 conformations, predicted six candidates, and the best reached a turnover number of 4,390, with time-resolved spectroscopy confirming the descriptors. The maths is in the bars: system efficiency is a product, so 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.2 is only 0.16. The weakest step dominates, in a leaf or a flask.

## 22. Closed‑loop discovery: predict, synthesise, test

[22:53–23:44] Does any of this give a real material? The strongest case runs the whole loop. A 2026 JACS study used machine-learning interatomic potentials to predict which dopants stabilise orthorhombic Sn3O4: boron, aluminium, strontium and yttrium. Hydrothermal synthesis and X-ray diffraction confirmed the phase, and aluminium gave the best visible-light hydrogen production. The institute's release quotes about 16 times the undoped rate, so treat that as a press figure; 5 percent aluminium was optimal in films. The older landmark is Burger's mobile robotic chemist: 688 experiments in 8 days over 10 variables, using Bayesian optimisation to pick each experiment, finding a formulation about six times more active. Predict, make, test, repeat.

## 23. Physics‑informed learning from small datasets

[23:44–24:39] The twist: photocatalysis has small, messy datasets. The animation is Bayesian optimisation: a Gaussian process gives a mean and an uncertainty band, and the next experiment goes where μ plus 2σ is largest, promising or unexplored. It finds the peak in a handful of points. Four examples. Nature Synthesis 2026: physics-informed descriptors cut the error from 4.70 to 3.31 and led to COF-343 for H2O2. Nature Communications 2026: a language model read 355 papers, extracted over 11,000 relationships and chose a thiazole linkage, giving 82.3 millimolar H2O2 without a sacrificial agent. Advanced Functional Materials 2025: active learning found a top hydrogen-evolution catalyst but predicted oxygen evolution poorly, an honest failure. And sparse models can work on tiny databases.

## 24. ML potentials resolve interfacial mechanism

[24:39–25:22] Finally, AI as a microscope for mechanism. Sumaria and colleagues trained a bias-corrected ML potential on DFT for CO2 on CuPt nanoparticles on TiO2 and used basin hopping, animated here: kick, relax on the ML forces, accept or reject with a Metropolis test. CO2 prefers the metal–oxide interface, carbon on platinum and one oxygen in a TiO2 vacancy, and the interface stabilises intermediates toward methane, consistent with experiment. It is the same logic as QM/MM on PSI: buy sampling that brute-force quantum chemistry cannot afford. The next frontier is ML for excited states.

## 25. Conclusions: ML succeeds when it encodes photophysics

[25:22–26:28] Run the zoom in reverse, back to the photon. It arrived with about 1.8 eV and roughly 6 ns. Light, not heat, made it an electronic isomer: a strong reductant with new selectivity. Antenna proteins funnelled it downhill, paying heat for irreversibility, in sub-picosecond hops by Förster coupling, with Dexter exchange guarding against triplets. The reaction centre trapped it in tens of picoseconds and walked the electron down a tunneling staircase, each step near −ΔG° = λ, with recombination pushed into the inverted region. When we copy that we fall short, and machine learning helps most when it encodes these same rules. Four takeaways. One: the clock rules; every step must beat decay. Two: yield is a product of steps, so screen whole systems. Three: predictions count only after synthesis and spectroscopy. Four: the frontier is ML for excited states and clean, shared data.
